# Product specification

## Objective

FretLab teaches music theory through the guitar. It takes someone from near-zero theory knowledge
to a practical understanding of intervals, chords, scales, keys and harmony — with every concept
anchored to a position on the neck.

It is not a digital textbook. Reading that a major chord is "root, major third, perfect fifth"
teaches almost nothing on its own; seeing C, E and G light up across the fretboard, then being
asked to build D major yourself, does.

## Target user

A guitarist who can play but cannot explain what they are playing. They know shapes, not reasons.
They want to improvise with intent, understand why a progression resolves, and find any note
without counting up from the open string.

## The core loop

Every concept in FretLab passes through the same six stages:

1. **What is it?** — a short, concrete explanation
2. **Why does it matter?** — its musical purpose
3. **Where is it on the guitar?** — visualised on the fretboard
4. **Can I recognise it?** — an identification exercise
5. **Can I construct it?** — a construction exercise
6. **Can I use it?** — applied in a practical guitar context

A topic is not "taught" until a user can do stages 4–6.

## Curriculum

Ordered learning path. Each module unlocks the next.

| # | Module | Covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Music foundations | Note names, twelve pitch classes, sharps and flats, enharmonics, semitones, why B–C and E–F are one semitone apart |
| 2 | The guitar | Strings, numbering, standard tuning, frets, the octave at fret 12 |
| 3 | Fretboard mastery | Locating any note anywhere, at five escalating difficulty levels |
| 4 | Intervals | All intervals by semitone distance *and* musical function, then found on the neck |
| 5 | Triads | Major, minor, diminished, augmented — built from a root, plus inversions |
| 6 | Chords | Sevenths, suspensions, add9, extensions, mapped to real voicings |
| 7 | Scales and keys | Major and natural minor formulas, scale degrees, relative major/minor |
| 8 | Diatonic harmony | Harmonising the major scale into I ii iii IV V vi vii° |
| 9 | Functional harmony | Tonic, subdominant, dominant; tension and resolution |
| 10 | Pentatonic | Minor and major pentatonic, all five positions, understood as intervals |
| 11 | CAGED | Five shapes mapping the neck, connected to triads and arpeggios |
| 12 | Arpeggios | Chord tones as target notes for improvisation |

Later: circle of fifths, modes, modal harmony, borrowed chords, secondary dominants, modulation,
substitutions, voice leading.

## The fretboard

One reusable component serves every screen. It renders whatever markers it is given and reports
which position was clicked — it holds no music theory of its own.

Display modes it must eventually support: note names, intervals, scale degrees, roots only,
hidden notes, answer validation, read-only display, and exercise mode.

**Visual semantics are global.** A root is the same colour in a lesson, in Fretboard Lab and in an
exercise. Colour is never the sole cue: markers pair colour with a label so meaning survives
colour-blindness and greyscale printing.

## Exercises

Generated by the backend from the theory domain, never drawn from a fixed question bank. The
generator picks a root and a chord type; the domain computes the answer; the validator checks the
user's attempt against domain rules.

Planned types: note location, single fret note, interval identification, interval construction,
chord building, chord recognition, scale building, scale recognition, fretboard intervals.

## Progress

Tracked per topic from recorded attempts: accuracy, mastery, study sessions, streaks. Practice
adapts over time — repeated failures on a concept increase its exposure. The mastery algorithm
starts simple and earns its complexity.

## Non-goals

- Tablature editing or playback
- A social network or content marketplace
- Replacing a teacher
- Generative AI features, until the traditional engineering is strong
